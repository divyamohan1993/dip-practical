Theory — Subtraction & Inversion

Image Subtraction (Absolute Difference)

Image subtraction computes the pixel-by-pixel difference between two images. For two grayscale images \( f(x,y) \) and \( g(x,y) \), the absolute difference is:

\[ d(x,y) = |f(x,y) - g(x,y)| \]

The result highlights regions where the two images differ. Bright pixels in the difference image indicate large intensity changes; dark pixels indicate similarity. This operation is fundamental to change detection, motion detection, and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) in medical imaging.

In OpenCV, this is implemented as cv2.absdiff(img1, img2), which avoids the underflow problems of direct subtraction on unsigned integers.

Image Inversion (Negation)

Intensity inversion, also called the image negative, reverses the gray-level scale. For an 8-bit image with \( L = 256 \) levels:

\[ s = (L - 1) - r = 255 - r \]

Every pixel is mapped to its complement: black becomes white, white becomes black, and mid-tones mirror around the center (128). This simple point operation is useful for:

  • Enhancing white/light detail in predominantly dark images (e.g., mammograms)
  • Creating photographic negatives for film-based workflows
  • Pre-processing before thresholding when the object of interest is darker than the background

A key mathematical property: applying inversion twice returns the original image, i.e., the operation is its own inverse (\( 255 - (255 - r) = r \)).

Mathematical Proof of Equivalence

An interesting property: the absolute difference of two inverted images equals the absolute difference of the originals.

\[ |(255 - f) - (255 - g)| = |g - f| = |f - g| \]

This means inversion commutes with absolute subtraction. We verify this computationally below by comparing the two difference images pixel by pixel.

Knowledge Check — Subtraction & Inversion

Test Your Understanding

Q1. Why do we use cv2.absdiff() instead of direct subtraction img1 - img2 for unsigned 8-bit images?

Unsigned 8-bit integers cannot represent negative values. When the result would be negative, it wraps around modulo 256. cv2.absdiff() computes the true absolute difference without underflow artifacts.

Q2. Applying image inversion (\( s = 255 - r \)) twice to the same image produces:

\( 255 - (255 - r) = r \). Inversion is an involution: applying it twice is the identity operation, returning every pixel to its original value.

Q3. If two images are identical, what does their absolute difference look like?

When \( f(x,y) = g(x,y) \) everywhere, \( |f - g| = 0 \) at every pixel. The result is a uniformly black image, confirming that the images are pixel-identical.

Q4. What is the relationship between \( |f - g| \) and \( |(255 - f) - (255 - g)| \)?

\( |(255 - f) - (255 - g)| = |g - f| = |f - g| \). The 255 terms cancel in the subtraction, so inversion does not affect the absolute difference.

Q5. In medical imaging, the image negative is particularly useful for:

Gonzalez & Woods cite mammography as a primary application. The negative makes bright tissue anomalies (potential concerns) stand out against a lighter background, improving radiologist detection accuracy.
